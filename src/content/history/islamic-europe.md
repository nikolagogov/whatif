In 732, near the city of Tours in modern-day France, an army of Frankish warriors under Charles Martel met a Muslim force from the Umayyad Caliphate. The Franks won. Charles Martel — "the Hammer" — halted the northward advance of Islamic armies into Western Europe.

Historians still debate how important that battle really was. Some argue that the Umayyads were already overextended. Others argue that Tours saved Christian Europe.

But what if Charles Martel had lost? What if the Umayyads had pushed further into France — and eventually into the heart of Europe?

> Would Europe have become part of the Islamic world?

The answer is not as simple as it seems.

## A Different Kind of Conquest

The Umayyad Caliphate was not a single, unified nation in the modern sense. It was a vast empire stretching from Spain to India, held together by political authority, religious institutions, trade, and an increasingly influential Arabic language of administration and scholarship.

When Muslim armies entered Spain in 711, they did not simply conquer it. They transformed it.

<figure class="article-figure float-right">
  <img src="/images/articles/history/islamic-europe/islamic-europe-cordoba.jpg" alt="Medieval Córdoba" />
  <figcaption>Córdoba in the 10th century — one of the great cities of the medieval world.</figcaption>
</figure>

Within a few centuries, Córdoba had become one of the great cities of the medieval world, renowned for its libraries, scholarship, medical learning, public baths, and vibrant urban life.

A victory at Tours would not have guaranteed the conquest of Europe. But if it had allowed the Umayyads to establish lasting control over southern and central France — and if their rule had survived the political upheavals of the eighth century — the history of the continent could have taken a very different course.

Not a single "Islamic conquest of Europe," but a slow, centuries-long process of cultural and religious change.

## What Would Europe Have Looked Like?

Imagine a Europe where Islam had become the dominant faith in much of the continent, Arabic the leading language of scholarship, and centres of learning in Paris, Oxford, and Bologna part of a wider intellectual world stretching from Córdoba to Baghdad and Cairo.

  <img src="/images/articles/history/islamic-europe/islamic-europe-university.jpg" alt="Medieval centre of learning" />

</figure>

This is not fantasy. The Islamic Golden Age produced some of the greatest scientific and philosophical achievements in human history. Algebra, optics, medicine, astronomy — all flourished under Muslim scholars between the 8th and 13th centuries.

If Europe had been part of that world, the trajectory of knowledge might have been very different. But it would not simply have become "the Middle East." It would have become something new — a hybrid civilization, shaped by both its Christian past and its Islamic present. Just as Islamic Spain was not the same as Islamic Persia, an Islamic France would not have been the same as Islamic Egypt.

## The Question of Religion

The most sensitive question is also the most important: what would have happened to Christianity?

In our timeline, the Umayyads allowed Christians and Jews to practice their faith in exchange for a special tax. This system — known as *dhimma* — was not equality, but it was also not forced conversion. Many Christians in Spain, Egypt, and the Levant kept their faith for centuries.

An Islamic Europe might have followed the same pattern. Some regions would have converted to Islam over time. Others would have remained Christian, Jewish, or something in between.

But there might also have been conflict — not necessarily religious war, but political and cultural tension. The history of Islamic Spain includes periods of both coexistence and persecution. An Islamic Europe would probably have been the same.

## A Different Kind of Renaissance

One of the most interesting possibilities is that a unified Islamic Europe might have produced a different relationship with classical knowledge.

In our timeline, the Renaissance drew on classical texts preserved, translated, and transmitted through both Byzantine and Islamic intellectual traditions. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 brought further Greek scholars and manuscripts to Italy, while the printing press later accelerated the spread of ideas across Europe.

An Islamic Europe might have followed a different path. Classical philosophy, mathematics, and astronomy could have remained central to its intellectual life, rather than being rediscovered through a series of translations and revivals. New ideas might have emerged from the interaction of Greek, Persian, Arabic, and European traditions.

Such a Europe might not have needed a Renaissance in the same sense as our own. But that would not guarantee an earlier scientific revolution. The institutions, political conditions, and intellectual debates that made modern science possible might have developed along entirely different lines.

## The Age of Exploration

If Europe had been part of the Islamic world, the age of exploration might have begun differently.

Islamic navigators had already developed advanced instruments like the astrolabe and the quadrant. Islamic geographers had already mapped much of the known world. Islamic merchants had already established trade routes across the Indian Ocean, the Sahara, and the Mediterranean.

A unified Islamic Europe might have developed the maritime ambitions and resources needed for Atlantic exploration. It is conceivable that an expedition could have crossed the ocean before Columbus — perhaps launched from Iberia by merchants or rulers seeking new trade routes. But advanced navigation alone would not have made such a voyage inevitable.

The consequences would have been profound. The colonization of the Americas might have taken a different form. The transatlantic slave trade might have emerged under different powers, taken a different form, or developed on a different timetable. And the balance of global power might have shifted in ways we can only imagine.

## A World Without "the West"

The most radical consequence of an Islamic Europe is that "the West" — as we understand it today — might not exist.

In our timeline, the idea of "Western civilization" is built on a specific story: Greece, Rome, Christianity, the Renaissance, the Enlightenment, the Industrial Revolution. It is a story of European exceptionalism.

If Europe had become part of the Islamic world, that story would not make sense. There would be no clear line between "Europe" and "the East." No clear line between "Christian" and "Muslim." No clear line between "Western" and "non-Western."

The modern world might have been organised around different cultural and political boundaries. Faith and language could have mattered more in some regions, while dynasties, trade networks, and territorial states might have shaped others.

## Would It Have Been Better?

This is where the What-If becomes a real question.

An Islamic Europe might have developed different political institutions, different religious traditions, and a different relationship with the wider world. The Reformation, the colonial expansion of European powers, and the conflicts that shaped modern Europe might never have taken the forms we know.

But that would not guarantee a more peaceful or just world.

A vast Islamic empire could have experienced its own dynastic struggles, religious persecution, wars of succession, and resistance to central authority. Christian and Jewish communities might have preserved their traditions for centuries — or faced periods of discrimination and violence. Scientific and cultural achievements could have flourished alongside political repression.

The honest answer is that we cannot know. History is not a moral experiment. It is a series of accidents, choices, and consequences — and every choice closes as many doors as it opens.

## What Charles Martel Didn't Know

When Charles Martel won at Tours in 732, he did not know he was saving "Western civilization." He was defending his kingdom, his family, and his faith. He was fighting a battle, not a historical idea.

What if he had lost? Perhaps the world would be more unified. Perhaps it would be more fragmented. Perhaps it would be more peaceful. Perhaps it would be more oppressive.

But it would not be our world.

And that is the point of asking the question — not to prove that one timeline is better than another, but to understand how much of what we call "inevitable" is actually just the result of a single battle, fought in a field in France, over a thousand years ago.
