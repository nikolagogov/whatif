Today, Mars is a cold, dry desert. Its atmosphere is thin, its surface is bombarded by radiation, and its water exists mostly as ice buried beneath the surface. But billions of years ago, that was not the case.

Ancient Mars had rivers, lakes, and possibly a vast northern ocean. It had a thicker atmosphere, warmer temperatures, and liquid water on its surface. For hundreds of millions of years, it may have been one of the most promising habitable worlds in the solar system.

Then something changed.

> What if it hadn't?

If Mars had held onto its water and its atmosphere, the history of our solar system would have looked very different. And so would the history of humanity.

## A Planet That Almost Made It

Mars is about half the diameter of Earth, with a surface area roughly equal to all the land on Earth combined. It has a similar day length, a similar axial tilt, and similar seasonal cycles.

  <img src="/images/articles/space/mars/mars-ocean.jpg" alt="Ancient Mars with oceans" />

In its early history, Mars had the ingredients for life as we know it: liquid water, organic molecules, and sources of energy. Some researchers believe that if life ever emerged there, it could have survived for hundreds of millions of years.

But Mars had a problem: it was small enough to cool relatively quickly. As Mars cooled and its global magnetic field disappeared, the solar wind and solar radiation helped strip away much of its atmosphere. Without a thick atmosphere, its surface could not stay warm enough to keep water liquid.

Over time, Mars froze. Its rivers dried up. Its oceans disappeared. What was once a potentially habitable world became a frozen desert.

If Mars had been slightly larger, or if its magnetic field had survived, that story might have had a different ending.

## A Living World Next Door

Imagine a Mars that never lost its water. A planet with oceans, rivers, and a thick atmosphere. A world where life — if it ever appeared — had billions of years to evolve, adapt, and spread.

What would such a world look like today?

Perhaps Mars would have forests and grasslands. Perhaps it would have its own complex biosphere, with ecosystems as varied as those on Earth. Perhaps it would have intelligent life — though that is a far bigger leap.

Even without intelligent life, a living Mars would change everything about how we see our place in the universe. It would suggest that life may not be as rare as we once imagined — and that Earth may not be the only place where chemistry has crossed the boundary into biology.

It would also mean that humanity is not alone — and never has been.

## Two Worlds, One Sky

If Mars had remained habitable, human history might have unfolded very differently.

For most of our existence, humans have looked up at the night sky and wondered what was out there. The red dot of Mars has been a mystery for thousands of years. But if that dot had been blue — if ancient astronomers had seen oceans and clouds on another world — our understanding of the universe would have been transformed.

Even with the simplest telescopes, observers would have seen clouds, seasonal changes, polar ice caps, and the shapes of continents and seas. The question "are there other inhabited worlds?" would not have been a philosophical abstraction. It would have been an observed fact.

The scientific revolution might have come earlier. The idea that Earth is not unique — that other worlds can support life — would have been obvious from the start.

And when the space age finally arrived, the race to reach Mars would not have been a race to explore a dead planet. It would have been a race to meet a neighbour.

## The Race for Mars

In our timeline, the space race was a political competition between the United States and the Soviet Union. The goal was prestige — planting a flag, winning a propaganda victory.

If Mars had been habitable, the motivations would have been very different.

A living Mars would have been the greatest scientific discovery in human history. It would have attracted the attention of every major power on Earth. Nations would have competed not just to visit Mars, but to study it, understand it, and perhaps even settle it.

A living Mars would have transformed the space race. Governments might have treated reaching the planet as a strategic priority, pouring resources into propulsion, life support, and long-duration spaceflight.

In such a world, permanent research bases by the 1980s would not be unimaginable. By the 2000s, humanity might even have established its first settlements beyond Earth.

But settling a living world would raise questions that no previous generation had ever faced. Would we have the right to change its atmosphere? To mine its resources? To build cities on landscapes that had evolved for billions of years without us?

## Would We Still Be Alone?

The most profound question is not whether Mars could have supported life. It is whether it did.

If Mars had remained habitable, and if life had emerged there independently, then life in the universe would be far more common than we once believed. It would suggest that life may emerge wherever the conditions are right — though two inhabited worlds would not, by themselves, tell us how common life is throughout the universe.

If life on Mars had evolved into complex, intelligent organisms, the implications would be even greater. We would not just be sharing the solar system with another species. We would be sharing it with another civilization.

What would that civilization look like? Would it be friendly or hostile? Would it share our values, or would it be completely alien? Would we have gone to war, or would we have built something together?

We cannot know. But the question itself is worth asking.

## The Mars We Have

The Mars we actually have is still fascinating — a frozen archive of a warmer, wetter past. Every rover we send finds new evidence of ancient rivers, lakes, and possibly even organic molecules.

But it is also a reminder of how fragile habitability can be. A planet can be born with everything it needs, and still lose it. A world can be habitable for a billion years, and then become a desert.

Earth could have gone the same way. We are only habitable because of a complex set of conditions that could have failed — and might still fail.

If Mars had kept its oceans, we might never have asked these questions. We might have taken habitability for granted, because we would have seen it twice.

But in our timeline, Earth remains the only world known to support life. And that makes it all the more precious.

## What Mars Can Teach Us

Mars is not just a planet. It is a mirror.

When we look at Mars, we see what could have been. We see a world that almost made it. And we see a warning about how quickly things can change.

If we ever go to Mars — and someday, we may — we will be visiting a world that could once have been alive. A world that reminds us how rare and fragile life might be.

What if Mars had never lost its oceans? Perhaps the solar system would be full of life. Perhaps we would have neighbours. Perhaps we would have a second home.

But in our timeline, we have one world. And that world is enough — if we take care of it.
