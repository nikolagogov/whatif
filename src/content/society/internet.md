In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee proposed a system for sharing information between computers. Within a few years, that system — the World Wide Web — had transformed almost every aspect of modern life.

Today, more than five billion people use the internet, much of it through the World Wide Web. It has reshaped communication, commerce, education, science, entertainment, and politics. It is hard to imagine the modern world without it.

But what if the Web had never been invented? What if the technology had been developed decades later — or never at all?

> Would we be better off, or worse?

The answer is not as obvious as it seems.

## A World Before the Web

For most of human history, information travelled slowly.

A letter might take weeks to cross an ocean. A scientific discovery might take years to reach another continent. News of a distant event might arrive long after it had already been forgotten by those who lived it.

Libraries, universities, and newspapers were the gatekeepers of knowledge. Access to information was limited not just by geography, but by wealth, education, and social class.

The web changed that. For the first time, anyone with a connection could access an extraordinary amount of information from almost anywhere, almost instantly.

Without it, the world would have remained divided into information islands — each with its own newspapers, its own books, its own television programs, and its own version of reality.

## Slower News, Deeper Focus

One of the most immediate consequences would be the pace of daily life.

Without social media, there would be no infinite scroll. Without email, no constant checking of inboxes. Without instant messaging, no expectation of immediate replies.

Communication would be slower — but perhaps also more deliberate. A letter requires thought. A phone call demands attention. A conversation in person cannot be paused or muted.

<figure class="article-figure float-right">
  <img src="/images/articles/society/internet/internet-library.jpg" alt="Library without internet" />
  <figcaption>Libraries would remain the primary source of shared knowledge.</figcaption>
</figure>

News would be published once or twice a day, not minute by minute. Public debate would move at the speed of print, not the speed of a tweet.

Would society be calmer? Would attention spans be longer? Would political polarisation be less severe? Perhaps. But perhaps not — humans have always found ways to argue.

## The Cost of Disconnection

But a world without the Web would also be a world of lost opportunities.

The web enabled millions of people to learn skills they could never have learned otherwise. It gave small businesses access to global markets. It allowed scientists to collaborate across continents in real time. It gave a voice to people who had never had one.

<figure class="article-figure float-right">
  <img src="/images/articles/society/internet/internet-global.jpg" alt="Global connections missing" />
  <figcaption>Without the web, the world would still be divided into information islands.</figcaption>
</figure>

Without it, many of the scientific breakthroughs of the last thirty years might have taken decades longer. The Human Genome Project, the rise of open-source software, and the rapid development of vaccines were all accelerated by increasingly connected global collaboration.

The web also created entirely new economies. E-commerce, remote work, digital art, online education — these would simply not exist in their current form. Millions of jobs built around the digital economy would never have existed.

And perhaps most importantly, the web gave ordinary people access to tools that had once belonged only to institutions. Anyone could publish. Anyone could organise. Anyone could be heard.

## A Different Kind of Public Square

In our timeline, the web has become the world's public square. It is where ideas are debated, movements are born, and revolutions are organised.

Without it, that square would look very different.

Political movements would still rely on newspapers, television, and face-to-face organising. Protests would be harder to organise at scale, but those who participated might be more directly connected through local organisations, unions, churches, universities, and political parties.

</figure>

The rise of populism, the spread of misinformation, and the polarisation of political debate might have taken different forms — but none of these problems would necessarily have disappeared. We cannot know.

But one thing is clear: without the web, the last thirty years of political history would have looked very different.

## What Would Have Replaced It?

Humans are natural connectors. If the Web had never been invented, something else would have filled the gap.

Perhaps a more advanced form of television — interactive, on-demand, but still centralised. Perhaps powerful commercial information networks built around telephone lines, cable television, and personal computers. Perhaps something we cannot imagine.

The technology would not have disappeared. It would have been delayed, fragmented, or shaped differently.

For example, in some scenarios, the Soviet Union might have developed its own closed network — an "internet" that only connected the Eastern Bloc. The West would have had its own separate system. The two might never have connected. The result could have been a fragmented digital world: a Soviet network for the Eastern Bloc, a Western network dominated by American companies, and perhaps other regional systems in Asia.

Another possibility is that the technology would simply have arrived later — perhaps in the 2010s instead of the 1990s. By then, the political and cultural landscape of the world would have been different, and the web would have grown in a different soil.

## The World We Have

The web we actually have is neither a utopia nor a dystopia. It is a tool — and like every tool, it amplifies both the best and the worst of the people who use it.

It has connected billions. It has also isolated millions. It has democratised information. It has also spread misinformation faster than ever before. It has created new economies. It has also destroyed entire industries.

A world without it would have been different — but not necessarily better. The problems we face today — political division, misinformation, loneliness, inequality — would not have disappeared. They would simply have taken other forms.

The web did not create these problems. It revealed them, accelerated them, and gave them new shapes.

## What We Owe to the Web

It is strange to think that the modern world — this vast, connected, chaotic system we live in — was born from a simple proposal written by one man in a Swiss laboratory.

Tim Berners-Lee did not invent the web because he wanted to change the world. He invented it because he wanted to make it easier for scientists to share their work.

He could not have imagined what it would become.

What if the web had never been invented? Perhaps we would be less distracted. Perhaps we would be less divided. Perhaps we would be more patient, more focused, more present.

But perhaps we would also be less informed. Less connected. Less able to understand the world beyond our own small corner of it.

We will never know. But the question is worth asking — because it reminds us that the web is not inevitable. It is a choice, made by humans, that we can still shape.

The web we have is not the only web possible. And that means its future is still in our hands.
